# Reflection & help-seeking

**Hypothesis:** Students who reflect more deeply — and reach for "just tell me the answer" less — learn more. Or grabbing the answer costs nothing. · **Target venue:** Metacognition and Learning

## Seminal / foundational

- Chi, M. T. H., Bassok, M., Lewis, M. W., Reimann, P., & Glaser, R. (1989). *Self-explanations: How students study and use examples in learning to solve problems*. Cognitive Science, 13(2), 145–182. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1302_1 — good learners spontaneously generate far more self-explanations than poor learners (15.3 vs 2.8 explanations), and self-explanation quality predicts problem-solving success. **Relation:** supports (founding evidence that self-generated explanation/reflection, not passive reading, drives learning).

- Nelson, T. O., & Narens, L. (1990). *Metamemory: A theoretical framework and new findings*. Psychology of Learning and Motivation, 26, 125–173. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0079-7421(08)60053-5 — proposes the object-level/meta-level monitoring-control architecture; monitoring and control can dissociate (monitor without acting, or act without discriminating). **Relation:** method/gap (the theoretical basis for treating "reflection" and "help-seeking" as separable monitor vs. control acts, rather than one construct).

- Winne, P. H., & Hadwin, A. F. (1998). *Studying as self-regulated learning*. In D. J. Hacker, J. Dunlosky, & A. C. Graesser (Eds.), *Metacognition in Educational Theory and Practice* (pp. 277–304). Erlbaum. — the COPES four-phase model in which metacognitive monitoring is "the gateway" to self-regulation; help-seeking and reflection are both control acts triggered by monitoring outcomes. **Relation:** method (framework for coding when/why a learner monitors before acting, i.e., before reflecting or seeking help).

- Baker, R. S., Corbett, A. T., Koedinger, K. R., & Wagner, A. Z. (2004). *Off-task behavior in the cognitive tutor classroom: When students "game the system"*. Proceedings of ACM CHI 2004, 383–390. https://doi.org/10.1145/985692.985741 — establishes "gaming the system" (rapid-fire hint requests / trial-and-error to extract an answer rather than engage) as a measurable construct, and links it empirically to lower learning; a machine-learned detector of gaming behaviour was validated against learning outcomes. **Relation:** supports (answer-grabbing/hint abuse measurably costs learning — direct evidence against the "costs nothing" alternative).

- Aleven, V., Roll, I., McLaren, B. M., & Koedinger, K. R. (2016). *Help helps, but only so much: Research on help seeking with intelligent tutoring systems*. International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education, 26(1), 205–223. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-015-0089-1 — synthesizes a decade+ of ITS help-seeking research; formalizes the instrumental (aimed at learning, reduces future dependence) vs. executive (aimed at finishing the task, minimal engagement) distinction, and shows instrumental help-seeking associates with achievement while executive/answer-only help-seeking does not transfer. **Relation:** supports + method (the canonical coding scheme for classifying a help request as reflective/instrumental vs. answer-grabbing/executive).

- Bjork, R. A., & Bjork, E. L. (2020). *Desirable difficulties in theory and practice*. Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition, 9(4), 475–479. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jarmac.2020.09.003 — difficulties that slow apparent performance during learning (retrieval effort, spacing, generation) often *improve* long-term retention, whereas difficulties that only add extraneous load do not. **Relation:** supports/frames (positions effortful reflection as a "desirable difficulty" whose short-term cost is the mechanism of its long-term benefit — the theoretical counterweight to "grabbing the answer costs nothing").

## Recent & directly relevant

- Bisra, K., Liu, Q., Nesbit, J. C., Salimi, F., & Winne, P. H. (2018). *Inducing self-explanation: A meta-analysis*. Educational Psychology Review, 30(3), 703–725. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x — 69 effect sizes, 64 studies; overall g = 0.55 for self-explanation prompts on learning outcomes, but weaker when the prompt only asks students to explain their own state of knowledge (metacognitive self-report) rather than generate content connections. **Relation:** supports + method (distinguishes *shallow* reflection prompts from *deep/generative* ones — directly informs how "reflection depth" should be coded and scored).

- Shields, M., Calabro, G., & Selmeczy, D. (2024). *Active help-seeking and metacognition interact in supporting children's retention of science facts*. Journal of Experimental Child Psychology, 237, 105772. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2023.105772 — children's ability to adaptively/actively seek help (vs. passively receive it) improves memory retention, and the benefit is moderated by metacognitive skill. **Relation:** supports, with a nuance/gap (help-seeking is not uniformly harmful — it interacts with metacognitive monitoring quality; sample is children, so generalizability to adult/university learners is a gap this paper can address).

- Guo, X., et al. (2022). *Using metacognitive prompts to enhance self-regulated learning and learning outcomes: A meta-analysis of experimental studies in computer-based learning environments*. Journal of Computer Assisted Learning, 38(3), 811–832. https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12650 — metacognitive prompting yields small-to-medium effects on both SRL activity (g = 0.50) and learning outcomes (g = 0.40) across CBLEs. **Relation:** supports (recent, quantitative base rate for how much prompted reflection typically moves outcomes — useful as an effect-size benchmark/prior).

- Authors withheld in source (2024). *Encouraging metacognitive reflection through prompts in a computer-based learning platform: Failure to find a benefit in a large-scale randomized trial*. Proceedings of the Twelfth ACM Conference on Learning @ Scale (L@S '24). https://doi.org/10.1145/3698205.3729547 — preregistered RCT, N = 1,005 seventh-graders: a reflect-before-hint prompt changed neither hint usage, redo usage, practice performance, nor post-test scores; low-prior-knowledge students used hints more but did not learn more from them. **Relation:** contradicts / null result (a large, preregistered study finding *no* detectable benefit of a reflection prompt and *no* cost of hint use for post-test performance — the strongest existing evidence for the "costs nothing" alternative and a direct scoop risk if not addressed).

- Choi, H., et al. (2025). *Reflection-satisfaction tradeoff: Investigating impact of reflection on student engagement with AI-generated programming hints*. arXiv:2512.04630 (preprint; field experiment in an online programming course). — two-trial field experiment: reflection prompts before/after AI hints, targeting planning/monitoring/evaluation phases; higher-quality reflections produced *lower* satisfaction with the AI hints received, i.e., a tension between reflective depth and learner-perceived help quality. **Relation:** supports + gap (shows reflection and help-seeking trade off against each other operationally in an AI-tutor setting close to this project's own AI-tutor context; satisfaction ≠ learning is exactly the confound this paper must not fall into).

- Divekar, R. R., Guerra, S., Gonzalez, L., & Boos, N. (2025). *Exploring the interaction-outcome paradox: Seemingly richer and more self-aware interactions with LLMs may not yet lead to better learning*. arXiv:2511.09458 (preprint). — controlled comparison of LLM-based vs. search-based help interfaces: LLM interactions were richer and more metacognitively self-aware but did not produce better learning outcomes than search. **Relation:** supports the "costs nothing" alternative from the opposite direction (richer-looking engagement with an AI helper is not sufficient for learning gains) — a caution against equating verbose/articulate help-seeking with genuine reflection.

- Lane, H. C. (2026). *AIED's unfinished mission: Centering agency and motivation in the age of effortless bypass*. arXiv:2607.05557 (to appear, AIED 2026 proceedings). — frames "effortless bypass" (using an AI tutor to skip the effortful step rather than to learn) as the field's central open problem, and calls for AIED systems to be evaluated on whether they protect learner agency, not just on immediate task success. **Relation:** gap (this is essentially the field-level statement of the exact hypothesis this paper tests, and a natural framing citation for the introduction).

- Authors withheld in source (2025). *Process mining measures students' help-seeking transitions when completing assignments in an online learning and assessment platform*. Metacognition and Learning. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-025-09442-0 — micro-level, trace-based analysis (frequency, transition, sequence) of 488 undergraduates' help-seeking behaviour; distinguishes struggling vs. non-struggling students by their help-seeking *sequences*, not just counts. **Relation:** method (published in the target venue itself; a directly transferable coding/analysis approach for turning raw help-request logs into a help-seeking-quality measure, as an alternative or complement to instrumental/executive coding).

- Pyke, W., Lunau, J., & Javadi, A.-H. (2025). *Does difficulty moderate learning? A comparative analysis of the desirable difficulties framework and cognitive load theory*. Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology. https://doi.org/10.1177/17470218241308143 — reconciles desirable-difficulties/retrieval-effort accounts with cognitive load theory by proposing that added difficulty helps on low-element-interactivity tasks but harms (overloads) high-element-interactivity ones. **Relation:** method/gap (recent theoretical account of *when* struggle helps vs. hurts — relevant boundary condition for interpreting a null or mixed reflection-depth result).

## Gap this paper fills

Prior work has established, separately, that (a) self-generated explanation and reflection prompts have real but modest average effects on learning (Bisra et al. 2018; Guo et al. 2022), and (b) executive/answer-seeking help requests correlate with weaker learning while instrumental help-seeking does not (Baker et al. 2004; Aleven et al. 2016). What is missing is a *joint*, within-subject account of reflection depth and help-seeking style as two axes of the same behaviour, measured from the same logged interactions with a single AI tutor, and tested against a genuine possibility that neither axis matters once prior knowledge and task difficulty are controlled — a possibility the 2024 large-scale null RCT (L@S) makes credible rather than merely a straw-man. This paper can additionally speak to the current, still-thin (2025–2026) literature on whether an LLM-based tutor's answer-seeking dynamics behave like classic ITS hint abuse or like something new (Divekar et al. 2025; Choi et al. 2025; Lane 2026).

## Scoop / contradiction watch

The 2024 ACM L@S preregistered RCT (N = 1,005) is the sharpest existing threat: it found a reflection-before-hint prompt changed neither hint usage nor post-test performance, and that low-prior-knowledge students who used hints more did not learn more from them — i.e., in that setting, reflection didn't help and answer-seeking didn't measurably hurt post-test scores. Divekar et al. (2025) adds a second, independent line pointing the same way (richer/more self-aware LLM interaction without better outcomes). Neither is a full scoop of this paper's design (neither pairs a coded *depth* measure of reflection with a coded *instrumental/executive* measure of help-seeking within one flipped-learning platform), but both must be cited and engaged with directly rather than left for a reviewer to raise — they are the strongest existing evidence for the paper's own null alternative.
