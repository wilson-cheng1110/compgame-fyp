# Does the flip work?
**Hypothesis:** Learning a concept by playing/experiencing it first, then testing, produces higher learning gains than testing first. A null result would challenge a popular teaching fashion. · **Target venue:** Computers & Education

## Seminal / foundational

- Hake, R. R. (1998). *Interactive-engagement versus traditional methods: A six-thousand-student survey of mechanics test data for introductory physics courses*. American Journal of Physics, 66(1), 64–74. DOI: 10.1119/1.18809 — introduces the normalized gain ⟨g⟩ = (post−pre)/(100−pre); interactive-engagement courses averaged ⟨g⟩=0.48 vs. 0.23 for traditional lecture across 6,542 students. **Relation:** method (the DV computation this paper's Hake-gain metric is built on).

- Schwartz, D. L., & Bransford, J. D. (1998). *A time for telling*. Cognition and Instruction, 16(4), 475–522. DOI: 10.1207/s1532690xci1604_4 — explicit instruction only pays off once learners have been "prepared" by prior exploratory experience with contrasting cases; unprepared instruction is much less effective. **Relation:** supports (theoretical foundation for experience-before-instruction).

- Kapur, M. (2008). *Productive failure in mathematical problem solving*. Cognition and Instruction, 26(3), 379–424. DOI: 10.1080/07370000802212669 — students who struggled on complex problems *before* a consolidation lecture ultimately outperformed lecture-then-practice students, despite failing during the initial phase. **Relation:** supports (originating productive-failure result).

- Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). *Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention*. Psychological Science, 17(3), 249–255. DOI: 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x — repeated testing (after study) beats repeated restudying on delayed retention, though restudying can still win on immediate tests. **Relation:** method/gap — the seminal testing-effect result the "test first" comparison arm has to be read against; complicates a simple "assessment-only = weaker" framing.

- Karpicke, J. D., & Blunt, J. R. (2011). *Retrieval practice produces more learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping*. Science, 331(6018), 772–775. DOI: 10.1126/science.1199327 — active retrieval/generation outperforms elaborative restudy even for conceptual, inference-level knowledge. **Relation:** supports (generation/active-engagement mechanism), but is itself about *post*-study retrieval, not pre-instruction play — a boundary to note.

- Bishop, J. L., & Verleger, M. A. (2013). *The flipped classroom: A survey of the research*. Proceedings of the 2013 ASEE Annual Conference & Exposition, paper 23.1200. https://peer.asee.org/the-flipped-classroom-a-survey-of-the-research — surveys early flip literature; finds mostly single-group, perception-only studies and explicitly calls for controlled designs measuring objective learning outcomes. **Relation:** gap (the methodological hole later meta-analyses, and this paper, try to close).

- Freeman, S., Eddy, S. L., McDonough, M., Smith, M. K., Okoroafor, N., Jordt, H., & Wenderoth, M. P. (2014). *Active learning increases student performance in science, engineering, and mathematics*. PNAS, 111(23), 8410–8415. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1319030111 — 225-study meta-analysis; active learning raises exam scores ~6% and cuts failure rates by ~55% relative to lecture, effects largest in small classes. **Relation:** supports (broad evidentiary base for the active/experiential component that "flip" and "play-first" designs both lean on).

## Recent & directly relevant

- Van Alten, D. C. D., Phielix, C., Janssen, J., & Kester, L. (2019). *Effects of flipping the classroom on learning outcomes and satisfaction: A meta-analysis*. Educational Research Review, 28, 100281. DOI: 10.1016/j.edurev.2019.05.003 — 114 studies; small positive effect on learning outcomes, no effect on satisfaction; effect strongest when in-class time isn't cut and when quizzes are added. **Relation:** supports, with moderators this paper's design should match (keep the check/quiz element; don't just reduce lecture time).

- Bredow, C. A., Roehling, P. V., Knorp, A. J., & Sweet, A. M. (2021). *To flip or not to flip? A meta-analysis of the efficacy of flipped learning in higher education*. Review of Educational Research, 91(6), 878–918. DOI: 10.3102/00346543211019122 — 317 studies; flipped classrooms beat lecture on 7 of 8 academic/interpersonal/satisfaction outcomes, d ≈ 0.20–0.53. **Relation:** supports (largest, most recent flip-specific meta-analysis).

- Sinha, T., & Kapur, M. (2021). *When problem solving followed by instruction works: Evidence for productive failure*. Review of Educational Research, 91(5), 761–798. DOI: 10.3102/00346543211019105 — meta-analysis of 53 studies / 166 comparisons / 12,000+ participants; problem-solving-before-instruction significantly outperforms instruction-first on conceptual understanding and transfer. **Relation:** strongly supports (largest quantitative synthesis of the exact ordering question, generalized beyond math).

- Pan, S. C., & Carpenter, S. K. (2023). *Prequestioning and pretesting effects: A review of empirical research, theoretical perspectives, and implications for educational practice*. Educational Psychology Review, 35, 97. DOI: 10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5 — being tested on unknown material *before* study (with feedback/study afterward) reliably improves later learning versus study-only. **Relation:** contradiction watch / gap — shows "test first" is not a weak-strawman condition; it has its own well-evidenced benefit mechanism the paper needs to address rather than assume away.

- DeCaro, M. S., Isaacs, R. A., Bego, C. R., & Chastain, R. J. (2023). *Bringing exploratory learning online: problem-solving before instruction improves remote undergraduate physics learning*. Frontiers in Education, 8, 1215975. DOI: 10.3389/feduc.2023.1215975 — 78 undergrads; explore-first group scored higher on the post-instruction assessment than instruct-first, despite lower accuracy during the exploratory activity itself. **Relation:** supports (recent, remote/digital delivery — close to this paper's platform context).

- Velić, L., & DeCaro, M. S. (2025). *Comparing effectiveness of exploratory learning activities given before instruction: generating multiple strategies vs. inventing one strategy*. Instructional Science, 53, 1537–1572. DOI: 10.1007/s11251-024-09701-8 — N=164; "generate multiple strategies" exploration beat instruction-first on procedural knowledge, but "invent one strategy" exploration did not differ from instruction-first. **Relation:** gap/nuance — the exploration/game activity's *design*, not just its position before instruction, determines whether the flip effect appears.

- Saba, J., Kapur, M., & Roll, I. (2025). *Learning about multivariable causality with interactive simulations: exploration before instruction may hurt immediate gains but benefits transfer*. Instructional Science. DOI: 10.1007/s11251-025-09726-7 — exploration-first showed *lower* immediate post-intervention performance than instruction-first, converging by the end, but produced better transfer to a new context. **Relation:** contradicts (on immediate gain specifically) / supports (on transfer) — a genuine boundary condition.

- DeCaro, M. S., et al. (2026). *Flipping a simulation before instruction can improve students' learning, interest and perceived competence*. British Journal of Educational Psychology, 96, 172–192. DOI: 10.1111/bjep.70007 — two experiments (N=168, N=357), undergraduate chemistry; explore-first and instruct-first tied on basic-facts recall, but explore-first scored significantly higher on conceptual understanding, transfer, interest, and perceived competence. **Relation:** strongly supports — the single closest prior test of this paper's exact hypothesis (simulation/interactive-activity-first vs. instruction-first, measured with a knowledge assessment).

## Gap this paper fills

Existing evidence for "experience/play-first beats test/instruction-first" comes almost entirely from single-topic, single-session lab or classroom studies in math, physics, or chemistry (Kapur 2008; Sinha & Kapur 2021; DeCaro et al. 2023, 2026; Saba et al. 2025), and existing flip meta-analyses (Van Alten et al. 2019; Bredow et al. 2021) bundle "flip" with video lectures and reduced/retained class time rather than isolating sequence as the sole manipulated variable. None of this literature reports a within-subjects, per-topic-randomized comparison across a large number of topics for the same learners, scored with a validated concept-inventory normalized gain (Hake ⟨g⟩) alongside motivation/interaction instruments. This paper adds that: a multi-topic (13-topic), per-participant-randomized FLIP-vs-CONTROL ordering test on one platform, letting the sequencing effect be estimated free of topic and cohort confounds and checked for consistency across many "mini-experiments" rather than one.

## Scoop / contradiction watch

DeCaro et al. (2026, BJEP) is the closest prior work to a "scoop" — it already tests the near-identical hypothesis (interactive activity before vs. after instruction) with two well-powered experiments and finds support, so this paper should position itself as a generalization (many topics, within-subjects, digital game format, concept-inventory Hake gain) rather than a first demonstration. Saba, Kapur & Roll (2025) is a genuine contradiction risk on *immediate* gain specifically (exploration-first underperformed instruction-first immediately, before converging and then winning on transfer) — if this paper's primary outcome is an immediate post-test rather than a delayed/transfer measure, a null or reversed result on H1 already has documented precedent and should be discussed as a boundary condition, not treated as a surprise.
