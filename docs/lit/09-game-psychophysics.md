# The game is the experiment

**Hypothesis:** Living a perceptual phenomenon firsthand — playing a game that IS the classic
experiment (Stroop, Fitts, Weber, Hick) — teaches the concept better than reading about it. Or:
the game is engaging but no deeper than a good explanation. · **Target venue:** Cognitive Science
(alt: npj Science of Learning)

## Seminal / foundational

- Barsalou, L. W. (2008). *Grounded Cognition*. Annual Review of Psychology, 59, 617–645.
  DOI: 10.1146/annurev.psych.59.103006.093639 — foundational theory: cognition is grounded in
  modal simulations of perception, action, and introspection rather than amodal symbol
  manipulation. **Relation:** theoretical base for why *being* the perceiver/actor in a
  psychophysics task could matter, not just reading about it.

- Glenberg, A. M., Gutierrez, T., Levin, J. R., Japuntich, S., & Kaschak, M. P. (2004).
  *Activity and Imagined Activity Can Enhance Young Children's Reading Comprehension*. Journal of
  Educational Psychology, 96(3), 424–436. DOI: 10.1037/0022-0663.96.3.424 — physical manipulation
  (and imagined manipulation) of story referents beat re-reading on comprehension/memory.
  **Relation:** supports — direct precedent for "doing/perceiving it yourself" outperforming
  passive text, though in reading comprehension, not perception/psychophysics.

- Kontra, C., Lyons, D. J., Fischer, S. M., & Beilock, S. L. (2015). *Physical Experience
  Enhances Science Learning*. Psychological Science, 26(6), 737–749.
  DOI: 10.1177/0956797615569355 — brief physical experience of torque/angular-momentum forces
  (vs. observation) improved quiz scores and recruited sensorimotor brain regions during later
  reasoning; replicated in a randomized college physics field experiment. **Relation:** supports
  — closest seminal analogue to this paper's design (self-as-participant vs. passive exposure to
  the same physical phenomenon), just in mechanics rather than perception.

- Fiorella, L., & Mayer, R. E. (2016). *Eight Ways to Promote Generative Learning*. Educational
  Psychology Review, 28(4), 717–741. DOI: 10.1007/s10648-015-9348-9 — reviews eight generative
  strategies (enacting, self-testing, imagining, etc.) that require learners to actively
  reorganize material rather than just receive it. **Relation:** method/framing — gives the
  vocabulary ("enactment," "generative activity") for classifying *why* playing-the-experiment
  might beat reading, and the boundary conditions under which generative activities fail.

- Chi, M. T. H., & Wylie, R. (2014). *The ICAP Framework: Linking Cognitive Engagement to Active
  Learning Outcomes*. Educational Psychologist, 49(4), 219–243.
  DOI: 10.1080/00461520.2014.965823 — orders engagement modes Interactive > Constructive >
  Active > Passive, each predicting deeper learning than the last. **Relation:** method/gap — a
  game-as-the-experiment plausibly sits at "Interactive," reading a description at "Active" or
  "Passive"; ICAP gives a predictive scaffold for the hypothesis and a vocabulary to report null
  results against.

- Mayer, R. E. (2004). *Should There Be a Three-Strikes Rule Against Pure Discovery Learning?*
  American Psychologist, 59(1), 14–19. DOI: 10.1037/0003-066X.59.1.14 — three decades of
  discovery-learning research (problem-solving rules, conservation, LOGO) show *guided* discovery
  beats *pure* discovery. **Relation:** contradicts (partially) — direct caution against the
  "just let them experience it" version of the hypothesis; motivates keeping explicit
  explanation/debrief around the game rather than experience alone.

- Alfieri, L., Brooks, P. J., Aldrich, N. J., & Tenenbaum, H. R. (2011). *Does Discovery-Based
  Instruction Enhance Learning?* Journal of Educational Psychology, 103(1), 1–18.
  DOI: 10.1037/a0021017 — meta-analysis (164 studies): unassisted discovery underperforms
  explicit instruction; *enhanced/assisted* discovery (feedback, worked examples, elicited
  explanation) outperforms other instruction. **Relation:** contradicts/nuances — reinforces that
  "the game IS the experiment" needs scaffolding (debrief, explanation) to beat reading, not bare
  exposure.

- Hake, R. R. (1998). *Interactive-Engagement versus Traditional Methods: A Six-Thousand-Student
  Survey of Mechanics Test Data for Introductory Physics Courses*. American Journal of Physics,
  66(1), 64–74. DOI: 10.1119/1.18809 — introduced the normalized learning gain ⟨g⟩; interactive-
  engagement courses averaged ⟨g⟩≈0.48 vs. 0.23 for traditional lecture, across 6000+ students.
  **Relation:** method — the field-standard pre/post gain metric this line of work should report
  learning outcomes in.

- Wouters, P., van Nimwegen, C., van Oostendorp, H., & van der Spek, E. D. (2013). *A
  Meta-Analysis of the Cognitive and Motivational Effects of Serious Games*. Journal of
  Educational Psychology, 105(2), 249–265. DOI: 10.1037/a0031311 — serious games beat
  conventional instruction on learning (d=0.29) and retention (d=0.36), but were *not*
  significantly more motivating (d=0.26, n.s.). **Relation:** contradicts the "engagement drives
  the effect" framing — cautions against assuming a fun game is a motivating one; the learning
  gain and the motivation gain are separable claims.

- Clark, D. B., Tanner-Smith, E. E., & Killingsworth, S. S. (2016). *Digital Games, Design, and
  Learning: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis*. Review of Educational Research, 86(1),
  79–122. DOI: 10.3102/0034654315582065 — digital games outperformed non-game conditions on
  learning (g=0.33) and augmented game designs outperformed standard game designs (g=0.34).
  **Relation:** supports — the reference meta-analysis for "does the game itself teach," useful
  as the base rate this paper's effect size should be compared against.

## Recent & directly relevant

- Skulmowski, A., & Rey, G. D. (2018). *Embodied Learning: Introducing a Taxonomy Based on
  Bodily Engagement and Task Integration*. Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications, 3:6.
  DOI: 10.1186/s41235-018-0092-9 — key moderator: embodiment only helps when bodily engagement is
  *integrated* with the task; high bodily engagement with no task relevance adds extraneous
  cognitive load and can hurt learning. **Relation:** gap/method — directly actionable design
  constraint: the psychophysics games must make the perceptual act itself the task, not
  decoration around it, or the manipulation could backfire.

- Macrine, S. L., & Lazonder, A. W. (2023). *Learning from Physical and Virtual Investigation: A
  Meta-Analysis of Conceptual Knowledge Acquisition*. Frontiers in Education, 8:1163024.
  DOI: 10.3389/feduc.2023.1163024 — 35 studies (2000–2021): no overall advantage of physical vs.
  virtual investigation (g=−0.14, n.s.); virtual investigation is *more* effective for adults than
  children/adolescents, and specifically when touching the real object/equipment adds no relevant
  sensory information. **Relation:** contradicts (usefully) — a digital game standing in for a
  live psychophysical stimulus is not obviously worse than the "real" apparatus, and may be a
  wash or better for adult (university) learners — softens the naive embodiment prediction.

- Kougioumtzis, K. (2026). *Embodied Cognition in STEM Learning: An Integrative Review of
  Conceptualizations, Mechanisms, and Boundary Conditions*. Frontiers in Education, 14:1811569.
  DOI: 10.3389/feduc.2026.1811569 — synthesizes four theoretical traditions (grounded/simulation,
  enactive/ecological, extended/embedded, pluralist) and four mechanism clusters (gesture-based
  representation, perceptual–spatial structuring, cognitive offloading, socially mediated
  interaction); flags the field as conceptually fragmented, with theory/mechanism/format routinely
  conflated. **Relation:** gap — names exactly the ambiguity Paper 09 needs to resolve for its own
  manipulation: which *mechanism* (if any) explains a "live the phenomenon" effect, versus mere
  novelty/engagement.

- Ma, W. W. K., Cheung, S. S. K., Li, C., Prayadsab, P., & Mungwattana, A. (Eds.) (2025)
  [chapter authorship as indexed — not independently confirmed beyond the publisher listing].
  *Enhance Learner Engagement Through Experiential Learning in a Gamified Simulation: A
  Longitudinal Study*. In *Blended Learning: Sustainable and Flexible Smart Learning* (ICBL 2025
  proceedings), Springer. DOI: 10.1007/978-981-96-8430-4_15 — a gamified simulation of Fitts' Law
  (built for an HCI course, n=68 undergraduates) significantly raised engagement — especially
  affective engagement — versus text-and-figures instruction, tracked short- and long-term.
  **Relation:** scoop candidate / closest prior art — same paradigm (Fitts' Law), same framing
  (gamified experience vs. reading), same population (HCI students); see Scoop watch below for
  what's still open.

## Gap this paper fills

Existing serious-games and embodied-cognition evidence establishes that (a) doing/embodying a
phenomenon can beat reading about it (Kontra et al.; Glenberg et al.), and (b) games in general
modestly beat non-game instruction (Clark et al.; Wouters et al.), but almost nothing tests the
strong, specific claim at the center of this paper: that *being the participant in a reconstructed
classic psychophysics paradigm* (Stroop/Fitts/Weber/Hick) — where the player's own
reaction time, error rate, or perceptual threshold reproduces the textbook result live — teaches
the underlying law better than an equally well-designed explanation or diagram of the same law.
The one close precedent found (Fitts' Law gamified simulation, ICBL 2025) measured engagement and
did not clearly separate "the game taught the concept" from "the game was more motivating," which
is precisely the ambiguity this paper's hypothesis is framed to resolve (engaging vs. deeper). This
paper also uniquely spans *four* classic paradigms in one design, letting it ask whether the
"living the phenomenon" effect (if real) generalizes across a perceptual illusion (Stroop), a
motor law (Fitts), a psychophysical scaling law (Weber), and a decision-time law (Hick), or is
paradigm-specific.

## Scoop / contradiction watch

The Fitts' Law gamified-simulation study (Ma et al., Eds., 2025 / ICBL 2025 proceedings, DOI
10.1007/978-981-96-8430-4_15) is the nearest existing work: same law, same "gamified experience vs.
text/figures" contrast, same course population (HCI students). It reports an engagement effect,
not a clean conceptual-learning effect isolated from motivation — so it constrains but does not
pre-empt this paper's claim, provided the write-up explicitly separates learning-outcome gain from
engagement/motivation (as Wouters et al. 2013 show these dissociate) and is explicit that this
paper adds Stroop/Weber/Hick and a reading-only control condition that the Fitts' study lacks. No
paper was found that runs all four classic paradigms as playable games with a reading-control
condition and a conceptual-knowledge post-test — that combination appears open. Flag for the
authors: search again closer to submission using each paradigm name individually (e.g. a targeted
"Stroop effect classroom simulation learning outcomes" pass) since a single-paradigm study could
still surface and should be cited rather than missed.
