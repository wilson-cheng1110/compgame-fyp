# Does it transfer to master's students?

**Hypothesis:** The flip's learning advantage holds for postgraduates as it does for undergraduates. Or it is specific to one population. · **Target venue:** British Journal of Educational Technology

## Seminal / foundational

- Knowles, M. S. (1980). *The Modern Practice of Adult Education: From Pedagogy to Andragogy* (rev. ed.). Cambridge Adult Education. — Founding claim that adult learners are self-directed, experience-driven, and problem-centered, requiring a different instructional design than pedagogy for children/novice learners. **Relation:** framework — the theoretical basis for expecting a population difference at all.
- Rachal, J. R. (2002). *Andragogy's detectives: A critique of the present and a proposal for the future.* Adult Education Quarterly, 52(3), 210–227. https://doi.org/10.1177/0741713602052003004 — Reviews the empirical record on andragogy and finds it inconclusive, fragmented, and hampered by inconsistent operational definitions. **Relation:** contradicts/gap — the "adult learners are just different" assumption behind this paper's hypothesis has weak direct empirical support.
- Sweller, J., Ayres, P., Kalyuga, S., & Chandler, P. (2003). *The expertise reversal effect.* Educational Psychologist, 38(1), 23–31. https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4 — Instructional techniques (e.g., heavy guidance/scaffolding) that help novices can lose effectiveness or actively hurt higher-prior-knowledge learners. **Relation:** contradicts/moderates — predicts the flip's guided Understanding-then-Assessment scaffold could help undergraduates more than postgraduates with more domain background.
- Freeman, S., Eddy, S. L., McDonough, M., Smith, M. K., Okoroafor, N., Jordt, H., & Wenderoth, M. P. (2014). *Active learning increases student performance in science, engineering, and mathematics.* PNAS, 111(23), 8410–8415. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1319030111 — 225-study meta-analysis; active learning raised exam scores ~6% (g ≈ 0.47) and nearly halved failure odds vs. lecturing, overwhelmingly in undergraduate STEM samples. **Relation:** supports — the headline active-learning effect this paper is asking "does it hold for a different population" about.
- Broadbent, J., & Poon, W. L. (2015). *Self-regulated learning strategies & academic achievement in online higher education learning environments: A systematic review.* Internet and Higher Education, 27, 1–13. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iheduc.2015.04.007 — Time management, metacognition, effort regulation, and critical thinking correlate with achievement in self-directed online formats. **Relation:** method/mechanism — self-regulated learning is the usual mechanism invoked for why postgraduates might respond differently to an unsupervised "learn first" module.

## Recent & directly relevant

- Van Alten, D. C. D., Phielix, C., Janssen, J., & Kester, L. (2019). *Effects of flipping the classroom on learning outcomes and satisfaction: A meta-analysis.* Educational Research Review, 28, 100281. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2019.05.003 — Small positive effect of flipping on learning outcomes across 114 studies; no significant effect on satisfaction; education level not resolved as a moderator. **Relation:** supports/gap — confirms the base effect this paper extends, but does not itself test population transfer.
- Chen, K.-S., Monrouxe, L., Lu, Y.-H., Jenq, C.-C., Chang, Y.-J., Chang, Y.-C., & Chai, P. Y.-C. (2018). *Academic outcomes of flipped classroom learning: A meta-analysis.* Medical Education, 52(9), 910–924. https://doi.org/10.1111/medu.13616 — Reports subgroup effects by education level: small positive effects for both postgraduate and undergraduate cohorts on some outcomes, but a near-zero/negative pooled effect for a graduate-level subgroup on others — critically, that subgroup rested on only ~4 studies (~789 participants). **Relation:** CONTRADICTS/moderates — the single most directly relevant prior finding; it is the closest thing to a "scoop" and the strongest empirical counter-signal to this paper's hypothesis, but is underpowered enough to leave the question genuinely open.
- Bredow, C. A., Roehling, P. V., Knorp, A. J., & Sweet, A. M. (2021). *To flip or not to flip? A meta-analysis of the efficacy of flipped learning in higher education.* Review of Educational Research, 91(6), 878–918. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019122 — 317-study meta-analysis across academic, interpersonal, and satisfaction outcomes (g = 0.20–0.53 on 7/8 outcomes); tests many contextual/design moderators and finds substantial heterogeneity. **Relation:** method/support — largest available template for how to test a level-of-study moderator rigorously, and the scale against which a single-institution UG/PG comparison should be read cautiously.
- Oliver, R., & Luther, L. (2020). *Flipping the graduate nursing classroom: An integrative review.* Journal of Nursing Education, 59(6), 305–310. https://doi.org/10.3928/01484834-20200520-02 — Reviews flipped-classroom studies specifically in graduate nursing programs; concludes the evidence base is low-rigor and the model is inconsistently operationalized across studies, preventing clean cross-study comparison. **Relation:** gap — direct evidence that postgraduate-specific flip research exists but is too heterogeneous to have settled the question.
- Talsma, K., Chapman, A., & Matthews, A. (2023). *Self-regulatory and demographic predictors of grades in online and face-to-face university cohorts: A multi-group path analysis.* British Journal of Educational Technology, 54(6), 1917–1938. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13329 — Uses multi-group path analysis to show self-regulation and demographic predictors of achievement differ in strength across distinct university cohorts (online vs. face-to-face). **Relation:** method — same target venue; a direct methodological template for testing whether a predictor/effect generalises across a defined population split, applicable to UG vs. PG.
- Tetzlaff, L., Simonsmeier, B. A., Peters, T., & Brod, G. (2025). *A cornerstone of adaptivity: A meta-analysis of the expertise reversal effect.* Learning and Instruction, 98, 102142. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142 — 60-study, 5,924-participant confirmation that low-prior-knowledge learners benefit from high-assistance instruction (d = 0.505) while high-prior-knowledge learners benefit from low-assistance instruction (d = −0.428); asymmetric, and moderated by educational status of the sample. **Relation:** contradicts/moderates — recent, well-powered evidence that a guided "learn first" structure calibrated for less-experienced learners may not transfer cleanly to a more expert postgraduate population.
- Yarkoni, T. (2022). *The generalizability crisis.* Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 45, e1. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X20001685 — Argues that verbal generalization claims ("this effect holds for X") routinely outrun what the statistical model (e.g., a fixed sample treated as if interchangeable with the target population) actually licenses. **Relation:** method — directly frames why "the flip works" claims from undergraduate-only samples cannot be assumed to license a postgraduate claim without explicitly sampling population as a factor, which is this paper's design logic.

## Gap this paper fills

Existing flipped-classroom meta-analyses either do not report an education-level moderator at all (Van Alten et al., 2019) or report one from a severely underpowered graduate subgroup nested inside a single-discipline (health professions) literature (Chen et al., 2018: ~4 studies, ~789 participants) — nowhere near enough to resolve whether the effect is population-general or population-specific. Dedicated postgraduate-only flip literature exists (Oliver & Luther, 2020) but is judged too inconsistently operationalized to yield a clean effect estimate, and none of it sits in a computing/HCI education context. This paper closes that gap with a same-instrument, same-schedule within-subjects comparison run across an undergraduate cohort and an added MSc section, letting population enter as an explicit design factor rather than an after-the-fact subgroup split — addressing exactly the generalization-boundary problem Yarkoni (2022) and the Talsma et al. (2023) multi-group approach both flag methodologically.

## Scoop / contradiction watch

Chen et al. (2018, *Medical Education*) is the closest thing to a scoop: it already tested a postgraduate-vs-undergraduate moderator in flipped-classroom outcomes and found a near-null/negative pooled effect for the graduate subgroup versus small positive effects at undergraduate level — a direct prior contradiction of this paper's hypothesis, though built on only ~4 studies and confined to health professions education. Oliver & Luther (2020) independently confirms the graduate-specific evidence base is too thin/heterogeneous to be conclusive either way. No study was found that runs a flip-vs-assessment-only comparison across UG/PG populations specifically in a computing, HCI, or general (non-clinical) STEM education context, so there is no in-domain scooper — but the medical-education counter-finding must be engaged with directly in the discussion rather than treated as if the question were untested.
