# How students take the tests

**Hypothesis:** How a student moves through a test — reaction time, hesitation, rushing, straight-lining — predicts their performance and engagement. Or the process traces add nothing over the score itself. · **Target venue:** Journal of Learning Analytics

## Seminal / foundational

- Schnipke, D. L., & Scrams, D. J. (1997). *Modeling item response times with a two-state mixture model: A new method of measuring speededness*. Journal of Educational Measurement, 34(3), 213–232. — First to model item response-time distributions as a two-state (solution vs. rapid-guessing) mixture; found the characteristic bimodal RT distribution that underlies almost all later rapid-guessing detection. **Relation:** method / seminal.

- van der Linden, W. J. (2006). *A lognormal model for response times on test items*. Journal of Educational and Behavioral Statistics, 31(2), 181–204. https://doi.org/10.3102/10769986031002181 — Introduced the lognormal RT model with person-speed and item time-intensity/discrimination parameters, fit via MCMC; showed it fits RT data far better than a normal model. Foundation for nearly all psychometric RT modelling since. **Relation:** method / seminal.

- Wise, S. L., & Kong, X. (2005). *Response time effort: A new measure of examinee motivation in computer-based tests*. Applied Measurement in Education, 18(2), 163–183. — Introduced the Response Time Effort (RTE) index (proportion of item responses classified as effortful vs. rapid-guessed); found supportive reliability/validity evidence and that RTE correlates with lower performance. **Relation:** supports (process signal predicts performance/effort) / seminal.

- Wise, S. L., & DeMars, C. E. (2006). *An application of item response time: The effort-moderated IRT model*. Journal of Educational Measurement, 43(1), 19–38. — Built response-time-based effort classification directly into IRT scoring; the effort-moderated model outperformed a standard 3PL model on fit, item-parameter accuracy, and convergent validity of proficiency estimates when rapid guessing was present. Remains the most widely used effort-moderated scoring model. **Relation:** method / supports.

- Kong, X. J., Wise, S. L., & Bhola, D. S. (2007). *Setting the response time threshold parameter to differentiate solution behavior from rapid-guessing behavior*. Educational and Psychological Measurement, 67(4), 606–619. — Compared threshold-identification methods (item-characteristic-based, visual inspection, and others) for classifying a response as rapid-guessed; a core methods reference for anyone setting RT cut-offs. **Relation:** method.

- Meade, A. W., & Craig, S. B. (2012). *Identifying careless responses in survey data*. Psychological Methods, 17(3), 437–455. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0028085 — Seminal comparison of careless/insufficient-effort responding detection methods: instructed-response items, consistency indices (incl. straight-lining/long-string indices), multivariate outlier analysis, response time, self-reported diligence. Still the standard citation for straight-lining detection. **Relation:** method / seminal.

- Goldhammer, F., Naumann, J., Stelter, A., Tóth, K., Rölke, H., & Klieme, E. (2014). *The time-on-task effect in reading and problem solving is moderated by task difficulty and skill: Insights from a computer-based large-scale assessment*. Journal of Educational Psychology, 106(3), 608–626. — Showed time-on-task effects flip sign depending on task type/difficulty and skill level: more time is associated with *lower* performance on easy/routine reading items but *higher* performance on difficult problem-solving items. Establishes that raw response time is not a simple effort or ability proxy — it's ambiguous between "actively working" and "disengaged" without complementary process signals. **Relation:** gap / important nuance against a naive process→performance mapping.

- He, Q., & von Davier, M. (2016). *Analyzing process data from problem-solving items with n-grams: Insights from a computer-based large-scale assessment*. In Handbook of Research on Technology Tools for Real-World Skill Development (IGI Global). — Treated action-sequence log data like text and applied n-gram feature extraction to find action patterns predictive of success/failure in complex problem solving; action-sequence patterns differed significantly by performance group and were consistent across countries. Foundational method for turning raw clickstream/process logs into predictive features. **Relation:** method / supports.

## Recent & directly relevant

- Kim, Y., Dykema, J., Stevenson, J., Black, P., & Moberg, D. P. (2019). *Straightlining: Overview of measurement, comparison of indicators, and effects in mail–web mixed-mode surveys*. Social Science Computer Review, 37(2), 214–233. https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439317752406 — Compares multiple straight-lining indicators across administration modes; straight-lining is stable across mail/web self-administration and negatively associated with education, but the relationship between straight-lining and true data quality is more complex than a single flag suggests. **Relation:** method / nuance.

- Rios, J. A., & Deng, J. (2021). *Does the choice of response-time threshold procedure substantially affect inferences concerning the identification and exclusion of rapid-guessing responses? A meta-analysis*. Large-scale Assessments in Education, 9(1), 1–25. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40536-021-00110-8 — Meta-analysis showing that different RT-threshold-setting procedures (arbitrary cut, distribution-based, RT+accuracy-based) largely converge in which responses they flag as rapid-guessed — a robustness result methods-focused replications should cite. **Relation:** method / supports (robustness of the core technique).

- To what degree does rapid guessing distort aggregated test scores? A meta-analytic investigation (2022). Educational Assessment, 27(4). https://doi.org/10.1080/10627197.2022.2110465 — Meta-analysis quantifying how much rapid-guessing contamination biases group-level score estimates in low-stakes testing, giving effect-size benchmarks for how much a behavioural signal (RT-based effort) matters for score validity. **Relation:** supports.

- Rios, J. A., & Deng, J. (2025). *Is effort-moderated scoring robust to multidimensional rapid guessing?* Educational and Psychological Measurement, 85(1), 134–155. https://doi.org/10.1177/00131644241246749 — Stress-tests the classic effort-moderated IRT model under multidimensional (construct-varying) rapid guessing and finds robustness is conditional, not universal — an important recent qualifier on the seminal Wise & DeMars (2006) model. **Relation:** contradicts (partially) / method.

- Welling, J., Gnambs, T., & Carstensen, C. H. (2024). *Identifying disengaged responding in multiple-choice items: Extending a latent class item response model with novel process data indicators*. Educational and Psychological Measurement, 84(2), 314–339. https://doi.org/10.1177/00131644231169211 — Adds process-data indicators beyond response time — text reread, item revisit, answer change — to a latent-class engagement model and gets better fit than an RT-only baseline. Directly supports the paper's premise that *how* a student moves through an item (not just how fast) carries information. **Relation:** supports.

- Tan, B., & Bulut, O. (2025). *The value of individual screen response time in predicting student test performance: Evidence from TIMSS 2019 problem solving and inquiry tasks*. Journal of Intelligence, 13(7), 82. https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence13070082 — Using ~13,800 students, found within-person variability in screen-level response time carries more predictive power for test performance than mean response time alone, i.e., the *pattern* of pacing across a test predicts performance beyond aggregate speed. **Relation:** supports — closely matches the "how a student moves through a test" framing.

- Aydin, F. N., Atalay Kabasakal, K., & Dilek, I. (2025). *Investigating the predictive performance of process data and result data in complex problem solving using the conditional gradient boosting algorithm*. Journal of Intelligence, 13(3), 29. https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence13030029 — Head-to-head comparison on PISA 2012 complex-problem-solving log data: process data alone gave moderate prediction of performance, result data alone moderate-to-good, and process+result combined gave the best prediction — i.e. process traces add incremental value but do not replace the score. **Relation:** supports (with an explicit ceiling — directly informs the "or adds nothing" arm of the hypothesis).

- Stupple, E. J. N., Pitchford, M., Ball, L. J., Hunt, T. E., & Steel, R. (2017). *Slower is not always better: Response-time evidence clarifies the limited role of miserly information processing in the Cognitive Reflection Test*. PLOS ONE, 12(11), e0186404. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0186404 — Found only a weak correlation between response time and accuracy on a reflective-reasoning test, and RT failed to differentiate correct from incorrect answers for 2 of 3 items — a direct empirical counter-example to assuming "slow = effortful/engaged, fast = careless" holds item-by-item. **Relation:** contradicts.

## Gap this paper fills

Prior work almost always tests one process signal in isolation — response time for rapid-guessing detection, or straight-lining indices for survey satisficing, or keystroke/revisit logs for complex problem-solving — mostly on large low-stakes international assessments (PISA, TIMSS, PIAAC) or one-off lab reflection tests. There is little work combining reaction-time, hesitation/revisit, and rushing/straight-lining signals from the *same* short formative/practice quiz taken repeatedly by the same learners over a course, and testing whether the combination predicts both performance *and* self-reported engagement/motivation rather than performance alone. This paper's process-trace-vs-score comparison, run longitudinally within one flipped-learning course rather than a single cross-sectional census assessment, is the gap.

## Scoop / contradiction watch

No single prior study runs the same design (multi-signal test-taking process data → performance *and* engagement, within one course, across repeated topic checks) — so no direct scooper found. But two lines of evidence argue against a strong version of the hypothesis and should be cited as counterweight: (1) Goldhammer et al. (2014) show time-on-task/response time is not a stable effort proxy — its relationship to performance flips sign with task type and skill level, so raw speed alone is an ambiguous signal; (2) Stupple et al. (2017) found response time barely correlated with accuracy at the item level in a reflective-reasoning task, and Aydin et al. (2025) found process data alone gives only *moderate* predictive performance versus result data's moderate-to-good — consistent with process traces adding real but bounded value, not replacing the score. Frame the paper's contribution against this "adds some, not everything" ceiling rather than a stronger claim.
